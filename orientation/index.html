Orienting your attendant

In CDS your attendant works for you, so Missouri asks you to orient them, and asks us to prepare you for it. It sounds official. In practice it is one honest conversation with the checklist below, ideally with your attendant present, and we will sit in whenever you want us to.

Ask us to help: 417-218-2888

Required by 19 CSR 15-8.400

The eight topics, in plain terms

Go through each one with your attendant. Check them off as you go, then both of you sign and date the printed copy. Keep it, and give us a copy for your case file.

  1. 1

    The basics of the job

    The schedule, your home, your routines, and how you like things done. This is the general orientation, and it works best with your attendant in the room rather than being told about it later.

  2. 2

    Electronic Visit Verification is mandatory

    Federal and Missouri law require every visit to be clocked electronically: with the mobile app, or by calling from your home phone. No paper timesheets. A visit that is not verified cannot be paid, so clean clock-ins are what protect your attendant's paycheck.

  3. 3

    What counts as fraud

    Clocking time that was not worked, clocking from somewhere else, one person clocking for another, or billing tasks that were not done. Fraud does not just end the arrangement, it is reported to the state's Medicaid audit unit.

  4. 4

    Allowed and not-allowed tasks

    Your plan of care lists what your attendant is paid to do. Tasks outside it are not billable, and hours beyond your authorization are not payable. Know the plan; work the plan.

  5. 5

    Falsifying visit records is reported

    Missouri requires us to say this one plainly: falsification of any visit verification record is fraud and must be reported to MMAC, the state's Medicaid audit unit. It is the one line in this orientation with zero flexibility.

  6. 6

    Your attendant's rights and responsibilities

    What they are entitled to as your employee, and what the job requires of them: reliability, respecting your direction of your own care, and honest records.

  7. 7

    Recognizing abuse, neglect and exploitation

    What each looks like, and that anyone can and should report concerns. Your attendant is often the person best placed to notice.

  8. 8

    The duty to speak up

    If your health takes a significant turn, or your attendant believes you can no longer direct your own care, they must tell us or the state. Staying silent is not loyalty, it is itself reportable.

Prefer to learn it as a course?

The full Consumer Orientation covers all of this and more in eight narrated modules with knowledge checks, at your own pace, ending in a completion record you can print. About 60 minutes, free, and you can stop and resume any time.

Also required: a backup plan

Every consumer must have an emergency and backup plan: who covers when your attendant is sick, and what happens in a weather emergency. We help you write it, and hiring a second, backup attendant through the program is allowed and encouraged.

Every year: pandemic preparedness

Missouri requires an annual refresher: we distribute our pandemic preparedness policy to every consumer each year, train you on it, and you pass it along to your attendant. We will prompt you when it is due.

The everyday practicals

Before the first shift, your attendant should know: how to clock in and out, that the care plan's hours are a ceiling not a suggestion, and that any manual correction to a shift needs our EVV correction form, not a guess. Our onboarding call covers all three.
